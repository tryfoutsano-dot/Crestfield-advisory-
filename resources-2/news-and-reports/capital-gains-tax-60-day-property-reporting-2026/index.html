PERSONAL TAX

Sold a property? You have 60 days to report it to HMRC, not until January

One of the most common and most expensive mistakes landlords and second-home owners make is assuming Capital Gains Tax on a property sale can wait until the Self Assessment deadline. It cannot.

May 2026 | Estimated read time: 6 min

Since 27 October 2021, UK residents selling a residential property on which Capital Gains Tax is due must report the disposal and pay the estimated tax within 60 days of completion. This is a separate obligation from the annual Self Assessment return. Miss it, and HMRC charges an automatic penalty before a single conversation takes place.

The rule most property sellers get wrong

The assumption that CGT on a property sale can wait until the 31 January Self Assessment deadline is incorrect, and it is the single most frequent reason property sellers face penalties. The 60-day clock starts on the date of completion — when the legal title transfers — not the date of exchange and not the day you moved out. Reporting is done through HMRC's Capital Gains Tax on UK property account, a separate online system from Self Assessment.

The minimum penalty for missing the 60-day deadline is £100, charged automatically. Further penalties and interest accrue the longer the report and payment are outstanding. These charges apply even where the eventual tax position is straightforward.

The rates from April 2026

Capital Gains Tax rates on residential property have not changed for 2026/27. They are the same as last year and have been in place since 6 April 2024, confirmed on GOV.UK: Capital Gains Tax rates.

Taxpayer band CGT rate on residential property
Basic rate taxpayer18%
Higher rate taxpayer24%
Additional rate taxpayer24%

Your capital gain is added to your other income when determining the rate. A basic rate taxpayer with a large gain may find part of it taxed at 24% because the gain pushes them into the higher rate band. The rate is not fixed by your salary alone.

The annual exempt amount remains £3,000 for individuals in 2026/27. This has fallen sharply from £12,300 in 2022/23, which means more property disposals now produce a taxable gain than in previous years. The allowance cannot be carried forward, so it is use-it-or-lose-it each tax year.

When you must report, even if no tax is due

The 60-day reporting requirement can apply even where no tax is payable. You must file within 60 days if any of the following apply:

  • You are a UK resident and the gain exceeds the £3,000 annual exempt amount before reliefs
  • You are a non-UK resident disposing of UK residential property — reporting is required regardless of whether a gain arises
  • Your total disposal proceeds exceed certain thresholds even where reliefs reduce the tax to nil — check the current HMRC guidance for your circumstances

Private Residence Relief covers the gain on a property that has been your only or main home throughout ownership. Where a property has been let, or was a second home, or was inherited and then sold, the relief may only cover part of the gain. These are exactly the situations where a 60-day report is required and where the calculation needs care.

The situations where advice avoids expensive mistakes

Buy-to-let and second homes

The most common disposals triggering CGT. The full gain is usually taxable after deducting acquisition costs, improvement costs and the annual exempt amount. The 60-day deadline applies.

Inherited property later sold

The base cost is generally the probate value at the date of death. Any gain above that value when the property is later sold is chargeable. Records of the probate valuation are essential.

Property that was once your home

Private Residence Relief and the final-period exemption may reduce the gain, but where the property was let or used as a second home for part of the ownership, the calculation is partial and needs care.

Separating couples

Transfers of property between separating spouses have their own CGT rules and timing considerations. Getting the sequence wrong can crystallise an avoidable charge.
